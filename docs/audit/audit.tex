\begin{trustbox}
\textbf{In one paragraph.} A Lean~4 development in this repository contains a
machine-checked theorem: \emph{if two simple heptagons in the plane have
finitely many boundary intersection points and share no vertex, their
boundaries meet in at most $38$ points, and $38$ is attained.} The proof has
been checked by Lean's kernel; it uses no \code{sorry}, no axiom of this
project's own, and no \code{native\_decide}. It is \textbf{not} a proof that
$f(7,7)=38$: the ``share no vertex'' hypothesis is the conclusion of \S9.1 of
Ackerman--Keszegh--Rote, which is assumed here, not proved. This document is
written so that you can confirm the first sentence and the last one without
reading Lean and without running anything.
\end{trustbox}

\section{Why you only need to read this document}

You have said that the mathematics now rests on the code. The natural next
question --- ``who has read the code?'' --- has an uncomfortable answer: nobody
outside this AI-assisted process has read or run any of it. This section is an
argument that for most of the code, that does not matter, and a precise
account of the part where it does.

\paragraph{Lean is a proof checker, not a programming language you must trust.}
A Lean file is not an argument addressed to a reader. It is a sequence of
instructions for constructing a formal proof object, which is then checked,
term by term, against a small fixed set of inference rules --- the
\emph{kernel}. If the file compiles, a proof object exists and the kernel has
verified it. If a step is wrong, it does not compile. There is no ``looks
right to me'' in between.

The consequence is the one that should interest you: \textbf{nobody reads the
proof files.} Not the author, not a Lean expert, not a referee. This
development is 1{,}779 \code{.lean} files and roughly 1.6 million lines, all
but a few dozen files being machine-generated case enumerations. Reading them
would take years and would establish nothing the kernel has not already
established more reliably. A Lean expert brought in to audit this repository
would not read the proofs either. He would do what \S\S2--6 of this document
do.

\paragraph{What a human must read is the statement.}
A proof checker verifies that a proof proves \emph{its own statement}. It has
no opinion about whether that statement is the one you meant. Everything that
can go wrong that a machine cannot catch lives in the definitions: if
\code{points9} means the wrong set, or \code{SimpleClosed} secretly assumes
general position, then a flawless kernel-checked proof establishes something
you did not ask for.

That surface is small. It is \ndecls\ declarations, fewer than sixty lines in
total, and the seven that carry the content are printed in \S2 beside the
mathematics they are supposed to say. \textbf{That table is the thing to
read.} It is the one place where this could be wrong in a way no machine will
find, and reading it takes ten minutes.

\paragraph{The three loopholes, named.}
There are exactly three ways to get something past the kernel, and all three
are visible:

\begin{itemize}
\item \code{sorry} --- a placeholder that stands in for a missing proof. A file
  containing \code{sorry} still compiles; Lean emits a \emph{warning}, not an
  error, and the build exits~0. This is the dangerous one, because a build log
  can say ``success'' over a hole.
\item A declared \code{axiom} --- a statement asserted without proof. This also
  compiles cleanly, with no warning at all.
\item \code{native\_decide} --- a tactic that closes a goal by compiling it to
  machine code and running it. It is not a hole and not an axiom; it moves the
  work out of the 5{,}000-line kernel and into the whole Lean compiler and the
  C toolchain beneath it. Vastly more code to trust.
\end{itemize}

All three are caught by one command. Lean's \code{\#print axioms} on a theorem
lists the unproved assumptions its proof depends on --- \emph{transitively},
through every lemma it uses. A \code{sorry} three lemmas down appears in the
headline theorem's own axiom list as \code{sorryAx}; a project axiom appears by
name; \code{native\_decide} appears as \code{Lean.ofReduceBool}. Nothing can be
buried. The expected output for this development is exactly
\[
  \texttt{[propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound]}
\]
which are Lean's own three foundational axioms --- propositional
extensionality, choice, and quotient soundness --- the standard basis of
ordinary classical mathematics, shared with all of Mathlib. Any fourth entry is
a finding.

\paragraph{What is left on trust when you finish this document.}
Three things, and they are worth stating plainly because they are all that is
left:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Lean's kernel} --- that the proof checker itself is correct. It is
  a small, heavily scrutinised program with independent reimplementations; this
  is the assumption every formalisation in the literature makes.
\item \textbf{That the statement in \S2 is the intended one.} You judge this
  yourself, by reading the table. Nobody can do it for you.
\item \textbf{AKR \S9.1} --- an external lemma, used as a hypothesis, discussed
  in \S7.
\end{enumerate}

Compare this with an ordinary paper. There, a referee must check the
definitions \emph{and} follow every step of the argument, and the second job is
the one that takes months and the one referees are known to get wrong. Here the
second job has been done by a machine. The residual trust is strictly smaller
--- item~2 above is the referee's first job, unchanged, and items~1 and~3 are
new but sharply bounded. That is the whole claim. It is not a claim that
formalisation is infallible.

\section{The claim, in ordinary mathematics}

\begin{trustbox}
\textbf{Proved.} Let $P$ and $Q$ be simple closed heptagons in the plane ---
seven vertices each, sides $[P_i,P_{i+1}]$ and $[Q_j,Q_{j+1}]$ for
$i,j\in\mathbb{Z}/7$, each polygon a closed curve that does not cross itself.
Suppose $\partial P\cap\partial Q$ is finite. Then
$\#(\partial P\cap\partial Q)\le 38$, and there is a pair for which the count
is exactly $38$; so $38$ is the maximum.

\medskip
\textbf{Assumed, not proved.} That no vertex of $P$ is equal to a vertex of
$Q$. This is a hypothesis of the theorem, written out explicitly; it is the
conclusion of \S9.1 of Ackerman, Keszegh and Rote, which is not formalised
here.
\end{trustbox}

\noindent Two remarks on what is \emph{not} assumed, because they are where a
formalisation usually cheats.

\emph{No general position.} Three collinear vertices are allowed. A straight
vertex --- where two consecutive sides are collinear --- is allowed. A vertex of
one polygon lying in the \emph{interior} of a side of the other is allowed, and
is the case AKR \S9.2 removes by a perturbation argument; here it is proved,
not assumed away. The only degeneracy excluded is vertex-equals-vertex.

\emph{Points, not crossings.} The quantity bounded is the cardinality of a set
of points of the plane. A tangential touch that does not cross counts as one
point; a point where three sides concur counts once, not twice. This is the
harder thing to bound and the thing the question actually asks.

\section{The gallery: hand-countable pictures beside the computed count}

The definitions in \S2 are set-theoretic. This section checks that they compute
what you would count by eye. \code{docs/audit/gallery.py} transcribes the same
predicates into Python --- \code{orientR}, \code{SimpleClosed},
\code{Adjacent}, \code{points9}, each annotated with the Lean file and line it
came from --- draws \galcount\ configurations, and circles and numbers every
point of $\partial P\cap\partial Q$. All arithmetic is exact rational
arithmetic (\code{fractions.Fraction}); no floating point is used for any
geometric decision. The tool self-tests 33 properties on startup and aborts if
any fails, including a sweep confirming its segment-intersection routine agrees
with the textbook orientation test on all 6{,}561 segment pairs over a
$3\times3$ grid.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Lead with the certificate.} Figure~1 is
\code{certificate\_38}: the configuration read from the file
\code{lower\_bound\_38.json}, the same object the Lean proof
uses as its witness. Its \certcount\ intersection points are circled and
numbered $1$ through $\certcount$. You can count them. That is the entire
lower bound, and it needs no trust at all.

\input{out/gallery_table.tex}

\medskip
\noindent The five \code{easy\_*} rows are sanity checks with obvious answers
(two convex heptagons overlapping in a lens: $2$; two crossing bars: $4$; a
convex heptagon and a turned copy: $14$, which is $2$ per side and the maximum
for two convex heptagons). The five \code{awkward\_*} rows are the ones that
matter:

\begin{itemize}
\item \code{awkward\_vertex\_in\_edge\_interior} --- a vertex of $Q$ lies
  exactly inside a side of $P$. This is the degeneracy AKR \S9.2 removes and
  this project proves; it is \emph{not} excluded by the theorem's hypotheses,
  and it is counted correctly.
\item \code{awkward\_three\_collinear\_in\_P} --- $P_0,P_1,P_2$ are collinear,
  so $P$'s base is one straight line split into two sides. \code{SimpleClosed}
  accepts this, deliberately. Any definition that rejected it would be smuggling
  in general position.
\item \code{awkward\_shared\_vertex} --- $P_0=Q_0$. This is exactly the case the
  theorem assumes away. It is drawn here so you can see what the hypothesis
  excludes.
\item \code{awkward\_tangential\_touch} --- the boundaries meet at one point
  without crossing. Count $1$: a touch is a point of the intersection. A tool
  that counted \emph{crossings} would report $0$ here and would be answering a
  different question.
\item \code{awkward\_overlapping\_sides\_infinite} --- a side of $P$ and a side
  of $Q$ are collinear and overlap in a whole sub-segment, so
  $\partial P\cap\partial Q$ is genuinely infinite. The tool reports
  \textsc{infinite} and refuses to produce a number. This is the honest failure
  mode, and it is why the theorem carries a finiteness hypothesis: without it
  the count is not a natural number and \code{ncard} would silently return $0$.
\end{itemize}

Each configuration carries an expected count written down in advance; a
mismatch, or a configuration whose polygons fail \code{SimpleClosed}, is
reported as a discrepancy and the tool exits non-zero. It exits~$0$.

\section{Sabotage: does the checker ever say no?}

A verification that cannot fail proves nothing. If a check passes on this
repository, the first question is whether it would also pass on a broken one.
The repository answers this with controlled mutations: take a throwaway copy,
introduce one specific defect, and require that the check rejects it \emph{and
names the right reason}. A control copy with no defect must still be accepted,
so that ``rejects everything'' is not mistaken for rigour.

\paragraph{L4 first, because it is the one that is not about broken proofs.}
Every other test below breaks a \emph{proof}. L4 breaks a \emph{definition}.
The mutation changes one character in \code{lean/AKR9.lean}:

\end{trustbox}

\noindent \textbf{Not one character of proof text is touched.} There is no
\code{sorry}, no axiom, no \code{native\_decide}; the mutated file is a
perfectly legitimate Lean development about the union of two boundaries instead
of their intersection --- a set that is obviously not of size at most 38.
\Lfourreq

Without this test, ``Lean proved it'' would mean only that \emph{something} was
proved. With it, the theorem is pinned to the definitions you read in \S2:
change what \code{points9} means and the proofs must stop type-checking.
\Lfourupshot

\paragraph{The three groups.}
\code{scripts/negative-tests.sh} has three groups. Group~N mutates the
evidence files (certificates, hashes, refutation proofs). Group~A mutates the
\emph{axiom log} and drives the repository's axiom checker over it --- these
need no Lean at all, because the checker is a log parser and can be tested the
way any parser is tested. Group~L is the four Lean-level mutations, L1--L4;
they require a real \code{lake build} of each mutated copy and are opt-in
behind a \code{--lean} flag.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Groups~A and~L were both run while this document was written},
and both transcripts are committed. The two tables below are copied line for
line from those runs, not transcribed.

\smallskip
\noindent One thing to know before reading them. \code{negative-tests.sh} can
print five verdicts --- \emph{accepted}, \emph{caught}, \textsc{not caught},
\textsc{not applied}, \textsc{wrongly rejected} --- and the generator that
builds these tables renders all five. \textbf{An absence of failures below is
therefore a fact about the runs, not a limitation of the table.} That
distinction is not rhetorical: an earlier version of the generator recognised
only three of the five verdicts and would have silently dropped a failing row.
That is the same defect as a checker that cannot fail --- the very thing this
section exists to rule out --- and it was found and fixed before this document
was built.

\paragraph{A correction, and what the axiom check really guards against.}
An earlier draft of this section asserted that \code{\#print axioms} prints
only when a module is actually recompiled, so that a warm rebuild ``replays
nothing and therefore prints nothing'' and only a cold build could produce this
evidence. \textbf{That was wrong}, and the committed transcript is what
demonstrates it: all \hlreplayed\ of its progress lines read \code{Replayed}
and not one reads \code{Built} --- it is a fully warm run --- and it

replays the cached messages of the modules it does not recompile.

a log in which the headline theorems never appear \emph{at all}. That is what a
bare \code{lake build} produces here, because \code{defaultTargets} omits the
\S9 modules, so those theorems are never part of the build and never print.
Such a log exits~0 and looks clean while establishing nothing whatever about
the statements you care about. Test~A4 in \S4 is exactly that case and the
checker rejects it. Its requirement is not that the build was cold; it is that
each of the six named theorems printed an axiom line of its own.

Mathlib (the Lean mathematical library, pinned here at \code{v4.33.1}) before
any of this project's own code compiles. The repository records about
\textbf{1.4\,GB} left in \code{lean/.lake} after a cold build, and flags that
figure as measured before later modules were added and not re-measured since,
so treat it as a lower bound. That download, not this development, dominates
the cost of a first build. You are not expected to do any of this; the point
of \S\S2--4 is that you do not have to.

\section{What is not proved}

This section is the reason to believe the rest.

\paragraph{$f(7,7)=38$ is not a Lean theorem.} The repository's own status
document says so in those words --- ``\textbf{\code{f(7,7) = 38} is still not a
Lean theorem}'' --- and the doc-comment on the theorem itself repeats it:
``\emph{This is not \code{f(7,7) = 38}, and it is not all of AKR \S9.}'' What
is proved is the statement in \S2, with the no-shared-vertex hypothesis
explicit, and with finiteness of the intersection assumed rather than derived.
That second hypothesis is not a technicality: the last row of \S3 is a genuine
configuration where it fails.

\paragraph{AKR \S9.1 is assumed.} The reference throughout is E.~Ackerman,
B.~Keszegh and G.~Rote, \emph{An almost optimal bound on the number of
intersections of two simple polygons}, Discrete Comput.\ Geom.\ \textbf{68}
(2022) 1049--1077. Their \S9 shows that
a pair of simple polygons may be perturbed into one where no vertex of either
lies on the other's boundary, without decreasing the intersection count. It
splits in two. \S9.2's half --- a vertex in the \emph{interior} of a side --- is
\emph{proved here}, in Lean, and is no longer imported; that is what
\code{points9\_le\_38\_of\_coincFree} accomplishes over its predecessor.
\S9.1's half --- a vertex \emph{equal} to a vertex of the other polygon --- is
still imported. It is carried as the single explicit hypothesis
\code{hcoinc} rather than baked into a structure, precisely so that a reader can
see it and check it against the paper.

As a \emph{statement} it is the kind a geometer grants on sight: coinciding
vertices, moved apart by enough to separate them and little enough to disturb
nothing else. Its plausibility is not in question. Its \emph{proof} is another
matter, and the repository's notes on \S9.1 are careful about this: AKR
establish it through their Theorem~17 and a dependence-graph argument about
which perturbations can be solved simultaneously, which is several pages and
not a one-liner. So ``routine'' here means ``believable without checking'', not
``short''. It is nonetheless \emph{not machine-checked here}, and the
project has found two genuine defects elsewhere in AKR's published \S9

\paragraph{Nobody outside this process has reviewed any of it.} Not the Lean,

\paragraph{What the complete argument therefore is.} Not a single artifact. It
is \textbf{a machine-checked theorem plus one routine lemma a geometer grants on

believable without checking, but roughly three pages of AKR to actually prove,
and not short. The repository's status document calls \S9.1 ``the one remaining

mathematics is actually assembled. But it is two pieces, of two different
kinds, and it should be described that way --- not as ``formally verified''.

\paragraph{And one smaller thing, so that the list is complete.} The gallery in
