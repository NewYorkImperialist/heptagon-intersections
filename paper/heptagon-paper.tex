\begin{abstract}
Let $f(m,n)$ be the maximum number of points at which the boundaries of two
simple polygons with $m$ and $n$ sides can meet. Dillencourt, Mount and
Saalfeld posed the problem in 1993 and settled the cases where $m$ or $n$ is
even; the odd--odd cases have resisted, and $m=n=7$ is the smallest open case.
We prove that two simple heptagons, neither having a vertex on the other's
boundary, meet in at most $38$ boundary points, and that $38$ is attained. The
hypothesis on vertices is a published reduction we import rather than prove, so
this is the case $f(7,7)=38$ \emph{conditional} on that import, not a
resolution of it.

The mathematical content is a reduction. Rather than enumerate the
$13{,}003{,}620$ candidate crossing patterns with $40$ intersections --- the
number of $9$-subsets of the $7\times7$ grid meeting every row and column, or
about $33{,}000$ after quotienting by symmetry --- we
exhibit \emph{two} local patterns, prescribing $8$ and $14$ side-pair
incidences respectively (the second together with seven self-non-crossing
conditions), and show that every candidate pattern with $40$
intersections and every one with $42$ contains one of them. Each of the two is
non-realizable, and a degree argument over $28$ cases, itself discharged by
machine, reduces the whole exclusion to those two facts. That discharge is not
hand-checkable (Remark~\ref{rem:cover-machine}).

Every mathematical assertion in \S\S\ref{sec:prelim}--\ref{sec:plane} is
verified by the Lean~4 kernel from primitive definitions, with no published
result about $f(m,n)$ appealed to. Two negative observations in
\S\ref{sec:e9attempt} are analytic and are \emph{not} machine-checked.
The statement we do not prove is the reduction to general position, for which we
import Ackerman--Keszegh--Rote \S9 and carry it as an explicit hypothesis. We are
explicit throughout about which assertions are machine-checked, which are
imported, and which --- notably the faithfulness of our definitions --- remain
matters for a human reader.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{The problem}

Write $f(m,n)$ for the maximum of
$\lvert \partial P \cap \partial Q\rvert$ over \emph{all} pairs of simple
polygons with $m$ and $n$ sides whose boundaries meet in finitely many points.
The finiteness restriction cannot be dropped --- two collinear overlapping sides
share infinitely many points --- but no further restriction is imposed.

We will also need a technical condition. Two simple polygons are in
\emph{general position} if no vertex of either lies on the boundary of the
other and no three of the combined $m+n$ vertices are collinear. This is
\emph{not} part of the definition of $f$, and the distinction is load-bearing:
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} assumes only the first half of general position, so if
$f$ were defined as a maximum over general-position pairs alone, that theorem
would already determine $f(7,7)$ outright and
Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction} --- the statement we import and do not prove
--- would have nothing to do. It is precisely because $f$ ranges over all
simple pairs that the reduction is needed.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Problem} (Dillencourt--Mount--Saalfeld~\cite{DillencourtMountSaalfeld1993}).
Determine $f(m,n)$.
\end{quote}

They determined $f(m,n)$ when at least one of $m,n$ is even. The odd--odd cases
are open. A 2012 preprint of G\"unther~\cite{Gunther2012} claiming $f(n,n)$ for
odd $n$ was withdrawn by its author, the stated reason being ``an unrecoverable
error in the proof of Lemma 2'', with a counterexample found to a weaker form of
that lemma. The smallest open case is $m=n=7$.

\begin{remark}[A caution for anyone searching the literature]
Several web search engines, asked about this problem, return summaries
asserting that the odd--odd conjecture \emph{is} proved. Those summaries
paraphrase the \emph{withdrawn} preprint. We mention it because we were
shown such a summary during preparation.
\end{remark}

Two elementary facts frame it. Each side of $P$ meets each side of $Q$ at most
once in general position, so $I\le 49$; and since $\partial P$ and $\partial Q$
are closed curves meeting transversally, $I$ is even. The published state of the art~\cite{CernyEtAl2003} gives $I\le 40$, and $38$ is
attained. So the entire question is whether
$40$ is attainable.

\subsection{What is proved here}

\begin{theorem}[Main, conditional]\label{thm:main}
Let $P$ and $Q$ be simple heptagons in the plane, each simple as a point set,
such that no vertex of either lies on the boundary of the other. Then
\[
  \lvert \partial P \cap \partial Q\rvert \;\le\; 38 ,
\]
and $38$ is attained. No condition on collinearity of vertices is assumed.
\end{theorem}

The hypothesis ``no vertex of either lies on the boundary of the other'' is
exactly the conclusion of Ackerman--Keszegh--Rote~\cite[\S9]{AckermanKeszeghRote2022}. We do not prove it;
\S\ref{sec:import} states precisely what we import and why, and
\S\ref{sec:e9attempt} reports an unsuccessful attempt to remove it.

\begin{remark}
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is \textbf{not} $f(7,7)=38$. Wherever that equation
appears in this paper it is written conditionally, on
Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction}. Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is the statement one
obtains after the general-position reduction has been applied, and that
reduction is imported, not proved.
\end{remark}

\subsection{What is new}

The contribution is a reduction, not a technique. Both ingredients we use ---
encoding realizability of a crossing pattern as an oriented-matroid
satisfiability problem, and refuting it mechanically --- are standard; see
\S\ref{sec:related}. What appears to be new is:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Two patterns suffice} (Theorem~\ref{thm:cover}). A search that
    was $13{,}003{,}620$ cases becomes two non-realizability facts plus a
    degree argument over $28$ cases.
  \item \textbf{The smaller pattern is human-readable} and needs no simplicity
    hypothesis: it constrains three consecutive sides of each polygon and
    prescribes eight incidences (\S\ref{sec:patterns}).
  \item \textbf{The reduction to the plane is unconditional.} The combinatorial
    argument is about orientation signs; getting from there to genuine point
    sets in $\mathbb{R}^2$ --- including removing the collinearity assumption
    that \cite[\S3]{AckermanKeszeghRote2022} assume ``for convenience'' and say
    can ``be achieved easily'', without proof (quoted in full in
    \S\ref{sec:import}) --- is carried out in full (\S\ref{sec:plane}).
\end{itemize}

A bounded prior-art search found no previous statement of the covering result.
We stress in \S\ref{sec:related} that this is weak evidence and not a novelty
claim.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:prelim}

\subsection{Configurations and the disjointness graph}

Let $P$ have vertices $p_0,\dots,p_6$ and $Q$ have $q_0,\dots,q_6$, indices mod
$7$, with sides $P_i=[p_i,p_{i+1}]$ and $Q_j=[q_j,q_{j+1}]$. For points
$a,b,c$ write
\[
  \orient(a,b,c)=(b_1-a_1)(c_2-a_2)-(b_2-a_2)(c_1-a_1),
\]
twice the signed area. Sides $[a,b]$ and $[c,d]$ \emph{cross} when
\[
  \orient(a,b,c)\cdot\orient(a,b,d)<0
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  \orient(c,d,a)\cdot\orient(c,d,b)<0 .
\]
This is a conjunction of \emph{strict} inequalities, a fact used repeatedly: it
makes crossing an open condition.

\begin{definition}[after \cite{AckermanKeszeghRote2022}]
The \emph{disjointness graph} $\GD$, whose name and notation we take
from~\cite{AckermanKeszeghRote2022}, is the bipartite graph on
$\{P_0,\dots,P_6\}\sqcup\{Q_0,\dots,Q_6\}$ with an edge $P_iQ_j$ when $P_i$ and
$Q_j$ do \emph{not} cross.
\end{definition}

In general position each side pair contributes at most one intersection point,
and exactly one when it crosses, so writing $I$ for the number of intersection
points and $\lvert \ED\rvert$ for the number of edges of $\GD$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:link}
  I + \lvert \ED\rvert = 49 .
\end{equation}

\subsection{Two unconditional bounds}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:even}
$I$ is even.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
$\partial P$ and $\partial Q$ are closed curves meeting transversally, so their
mod-$2$ intersection number is $0$: each side of $P$ contributes to $I$ once per
transversal crossing, and summing the crossing parities around the closed walk
$\partial P$ returns to the starting value. Hence $I\equiv 0 \pmod 2$. The
formal argument is carried out on the orientation data, so the Jordan curve
theorem is not used --- neither here nor anywhere else in this paper.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:42}
$\delta(\GD)\ge 1$, and consequently $I\le 42$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix a side $P_i$ and let $\ell$ be the line through it. Since $\partial Q$ is a
closed curve, $\ell$ meets it an even number of times, so $\ell$ cannot cross
all seven sides of $Q$; in particular $P_i$ fails to cross at least one $Q_j$.
The same holds with the roles exchanged, so every one of the fourteen vertices
of $\GD$ has degree at least~$1$ and $\lvert \ED\rvert\ge 7$. With
\eqref{eq:link} this gives $I\le 42$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Where simplicity is and is not needed]
Lemma~\ref{lem:42} does \emph{not} need simplicity, and it is worth being
precise about why, because the neighbouring corollaries do.

The argument above uses only that $\partial Q$ is a \emph{closed} walk, which is
true whether or not it crosses itself. So $\delta(\GD)\ge1$ and $I\le 42$ hold for
any closed polygons in general position.

What does need simplicity is the \emph{exclusion} of $40$ and $42$, because
Theorem~\ref{thm:pat14}'s seven self-non-crossing conditions are supplied by it.
Here is a witness, and note carefully what it does and does not show: the
$14$ points
\[
\begin{aligned}
P&:(197,86),(182,258),(152,128),(269,227),(90,125),(278,210),(132,227),\\
Q&:(149,267),(228,177),(105,155),(278,223),(38,63),(253,284),(184,98)
\end{aligned}
\]
have all $364$ orientation determinants nonzero and satisfy $I=42$; both
heptagons are self-crossing, and $\GD$ is a perfect matching. This does
\emph{not} contradict Lemma~\ref{lem:42} --- it \emph{attains} the bound. What it
contradicts is Corollary~\ref{cor:not4042} read without a simplicity hypothesis,
which is why that hypothesis appears there and in Corollary~\ref{cor:38}.
Lemma~\ref{lem:even}, like Lemma~\ref{lem:42}, needs no simplicity at all.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
\v{C}ern\'y et al.~\cite[Lemma~1]{CernyEtAl2003} obtain $\delta(\GD)\ge1$ from the fact that a
line meets a simple $7$-gon in at most $6$ sides. We do not import that: the
minimum-degree statement is derived directly and is machine-checked
unconditionally, so their Lemma~1 is corroboration here, not a dependency.
\end{remark}

By Lemmas~\ref{lem:even} and~\ref{lem:42}, $I\in\{\dots,38,40,42\}$, and since
$38$ is attained (\S\ref{sec:lower}) the theorem reduces to excluding $40$ and
$42$.

\subsection{Orientation data}

All of \S\S\ref{sec:patterns}--\ref{sec:exclude} concerns only the signs of the
$\binom{14}{3}=364$ determinants $\orient(v_a,v_b,v_c)$ on the combined vertex
set. A \emph{configuration} is a $14$-tuple of points together with the
requirement that all $364$ signs be nonzero; a \emph{crossing pattern} is the
resulting $7\times7$ Boolean matrix of side-pair crossings. Realizability
questions about patterns are therefore questions about which sign vectors arise
from actual point sets --- that is, about chirotopes.

\section{Two forbidden patterns}\label{sec:patterns}

The plan for the next two sections: we exhibit two local crossing patterns and
show in \S\ref{sec:howproved} that neither is realizable; then \S\ref{sec:cover}
shows that every crossing pattern with $I\in\{40,42\}$ contains one of them. The
two patterns were \emph{found} by computer search --- minimising unsatisfiable
cores --- but what they are is small enough to state in a table, and $\Pi_8$ is
small enough to check by hand.

\subsection{The small pattern}

\begin{definition}\label{def:pat8}
Pattern $\Pi_8$ constrains sides $P_0,P_1,P_2$ and $Q_0,Q_1,Q_2$:
\[
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
      & Q_0 & Q_1 & Q_2 \\ \hline
  P_0 & \times & \times & \times \\
  P_1 & \times & \cdot   & \times \\
  P_2 & \times & \times & \ast
\end{array}
\]
where $\times$ means \emph{crosses}, $\cdot$ means \emph{does not cross}, and
$\ast$ is unconstrained. That is eight prescribed incidences: seven crossings
and one non-crossing.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:pat8}
No configuration realizes $\Pi_8$.
\end{theorem}

Theorem~\ref{thm:pat8} assumes nothing beyond the eight incidences and the
nondegeneracy of the $364$ determinants: in particular it needs \emph{no}
hypothesis that $P$ or $Q$ is simple. It is the single most compact
\emph{statement} in this work.

We must be equally plain about the other half: \textbf{we offer no
human-surveyable proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:pat8} or of
Theorem~\ref{thm:pat14}.} What is small enough to check by hand is the
\emph{pattern}, not its non-realizability. A reader who wants to attack the
result should attack $\Pi_8$: eight incidences on three consecutive sides of
each polygon is a small enough object that a synthetic argument, or a
counterexample, may well be findable by a specialist. We could not find either.

What we can offer instead is an independent verification by a completely
different route, described next.

\subsection{An independent check of \texorpdfstring{$\Pi_8$}{Pi 8} by order
types}\label{sec:ot8}

$\Pi_8$ constrains three consecutive sides of each polygon, so it involves
exactly \textbf{eight points}, $P_0,\dots,P_3$ and $Q_0,\dots,Q_3$. Whether two
segments cross is determined by orientation signs, hence by the \emph{order
type} of those eight points --- and the order types of eight points in general
position are completely enumerated: there are $3315$ of them
\cite{AichholzerAurenhammerKrasser2002,OrderTypeDatabase}. So
Theorem~\ref{thm:pat8} can be decided by enumeration, with no solver and no
proof assistant.

We did this. Checking every labelling of every one of the $3315$ representatives:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{no order type of eight points admits $\Pi_8$ --- $0$ of $3315$.}
\end{quote}
Two conventions might have left a gap and neither does. The database identifies
mirror images, which is harmless here because reflection negates every
orientation while each crossing test is a \emph{product} of two, so the crossing
relation is unchanged. And it stores one representative per order type, so the
search enumerates labellings itself.

\begin{remark}[Controls, and a by-product]\label{rem:ot8controls}
``We found nothing'' is worth nothing without a control that the search can find
something. Two were run. The crossing pattern taken from the certificate of
\S\ref{sec:lower} \emph{is} located in the database. Sharper: dropping any
single one of $\Pi_8$'s eight conditions yields a pattern that \emph{is}
realizable, and all eight relaxations are found. That rules out a vacuously
failing search, and as a by-product it confirms \textbf{$\Pi_8$ is minimal} ---
a property we had previously established only by randomised minimisation of
unsatisfiable cores, i.e.\ by the very machinery this check is meant to be
independent of.
\end{remark}

This is corroboration, not a second proof: it is independent of the Lean route
in its source of truth (an externally published database), its algorithm
(enumeration, not refutation), and its implementation.

\begin{remark}[The same check cannot be run for $\Pi_{14}$]\label{rem:no14}
$\Pi_{14}$ constrains four consecutive sides of $P$ and five of $Q$, so it
involves $5+6=\mathbf{11}$ points, not eight. The enumeration of order types is
complete only to $n=10$ --- and already $14{,}309{,}547$ there --- so this route
is unavailable for $\Pi_{14}$ and we do not have an independent exhaustive check
of Theorem~\ref{thm:pat14}. Nor does the minimality of $\Pi_{14}$ offer a way
round: every proper subset of its conditions is realizable, so there is no
smaller non-realizable sub-pattern to catch on fewer points.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The larger pattern}

\begin{definition}\label{def:pat14}
Pattern $\Pi_{14}$ constrains $P_0,\dots,P_3$ and $Q_0,\dots,Q_4$ by fourteen
incidences,
\[
\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
      & Q_0 & Q_1 & Q_2 & Q_3 & Q_4 \\ \hline
  P_0 & \ast & \times & \times & \ast & \times \\
  P_1 & \times & \cdot & \times & \times & \times \\
  P_2 & \times & \times & \ast & \ast & \times \\
  P_3 & \times & \times & \times & \ast & \ast
\end{array}
\]
together with seven simplicity conditions: $P_0\!\not\times\!P_2$,
$P_0\!\not\times\!P_3$, $P_1\!\not\times\!P_3$, $Q_0\!\not\times\!Q_2$,
$Q_0\!\not\times\!Q_3$, $Q_1\!\not\times\!Q_3$, $Q_1\!\not\times\!Q_4$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:pat14}
No configuration realizes $\Pi_{14}$.
\end{theorem}

Unlike $\Pi_8$, this one genuinely uses that the polygons do not cross
themselves; the seven self-non-crossing conditions are part of the hypothesis
and are supplied by simplicity.

\subsection{How the patterns are proved non-realizable}\label{sec:howproved}

Both theorems are established by encoding realizability as a Boolean
satisfiability problem over the $364$ orientation variables --- Grassmann--Pl\"ucker
relations, acyclicity, and the prescribed incidences --- and refuting it. The
refutation is not trusted:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Each clause of the encoding is \emph{derived from the geometry} inside
    the proof assistant. The Grassmann--Pl\"ucker and acyclicity clauses are
    polynomial identities in the coordinates; the pattern clauses are the
    definition of crossing unfolded. Nothing about the encoding is assumed.
  \item The solver's refutation is checked, then transcribed into an explicit
    chain of unit-propagation steps. The kernel verifies a chain of
    implications between propositions; \emph{no search runs inside the
    proof assistant}.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}[Controls against a vacuous encoding]\label{rem:controls}
The obvious worry is an over-constrained encoding, unsatisfiable for
\emph{everything}, which would refute the patterns for the wrong reason. Two
things guard against it.

The substantive one: because every clause is \emph{derived} from the geometry
inside the proof assistant rather than asserted, a clause that did not in fact
follow could not be proved at all. The general-position clauses are theorems
about arbitrary configurations and they are satisfiable --- the configuration of
\S\ref{sec:lower} has all $364$ determinants nonzero, checked by exact
arithmetic. So unsatisfiability cannot come from the general-position part; it
must come from the prescribed incidences.

A cheaper check: the crossing matrix of the $38$-crossing configuration of
\S\ref{sec:lower} contains \emph{no} relabelled copy of either pattern. Note
what has to be checked here --- containment in Theorem~\ref{thm:cover} is up to
the order-$392$ group, so observing that the identity labelling fails would
establish nothing; all $588$ instances must be excluded, and they are. This is a
\emph{negative} check that a vacuously unsatisfiable encoding would pass
trivially; it is the first argument, not this one, that addresses that failure
mode.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Soundness, not completeness, is what is needed]
The encoding is a set of \emph{necessary} conditions for realizability. That is
the right direction here: we are proving non-realizability, so we need only that
every clause is valid. Completeness of the encoding --- that every sign vector
satisfying the clauses is realizable, which is false in general --- is neither
claimed nor used.
\end{remark}

\section{The covering theorem}\label{sec:cover}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:cover}
Every crossing pattern of two simple heptagons with $I=40$, and every one with
$I=42$, contains $\Pi_8$ or $\Pi_{14}$ after relabelling.
\end{theorem}

Two definitions are needed to read this statement.

A configuration \emph{realizes} a pattern if its crossing relation agrees with
every prescribed entry; entries marked $\ast$ are unconstrained. A crossing
pattern \emph{contains} $\Pi$ if some relabelling carries $\Pi$ onto a block of
cyclically consecutive side indices of each polygon, on which all the
prescribed entries hold.

``Relabelling'' is the group of order $392=14\cdot 14\cdot 2$ generated by
rotating and reflecting each polygon independently and exchanging $P$ with $Q$
(legitimate only because $m=n$). On \emph{side} indices a vertex rotation by $k$
sends $P_i$ to $P_{i+k}$, whereas the reflection $p_i\mapsto p_{-i}$ sends $P_i$
to $P_{-i-1}$; the shift matters when checking a containment by hand.

\subsection{The $I=42$ case}

By \eqref{eq:link}, $I=42$ forces $\lvert \ED\rvert=7$, and with
$\delta(\GD)\ge1$ on both sides the graph is a perfect matching. There are
$7!=5040$ of these, falling into $28$ orbits under the order-$392$ group. That
every one of them contains $\Pi_8$ or $\Pi_{14}$ is \emph{machine-checked}, by a
propagation chain of the same kind as Remark~\ref{rem:cover-machine} describes.
It is not a hand argument, and the brevity of this paragraph should not suggest
otherwise. (The coincidence that $28$ also counts the degree vectors of
\S\ref{sec:cover} below is exactly that --- the two have nothing to do with each
other.)

\subsection{The $I=40$ case, and its 28 degree shapes}

Here $\lvert \ED\rvert=9$, so $\GD$ has nine edges with all fourteen degrees at
least~$1$. Write $r_i=\deg P_i$, so $\sum_{i}r_i=9$ with every $r_i\ge1$.
Subtracting one from each, the excess is $2$ distributed over seven rows, so
either

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item one row has degree $3$ and the rest degree $1$ --- $7$ placements; or
  \item two rows have degree $2$ and the rest degree $1$ --- $\binom{7}{2}=21$
    placements, which split by the cyclic distance $1$, $2$ or $3$ between the
    two rows into $7+7+7$.
\end{itemize}

That is $7+21=\mathbf{28}$ degree vectors, and up to rotation of $P$ they are
\textbf{four} shapes. The same applies to columns, and the column degrees enter
through the constraint $\sum_j c_j=9$ with $c_j\ge1$.

\begin{remark}[What is and is not human-sized here]\label{rem:cover-machine}
The enumeration above is human-checkable and we have just done it. \textbf{The
discharge of each of the 28 cases is not.} Each case is settled by a machine
propagation chain in the same style as \S\ref{sec:howproved}, drawing on a
library of $588$ pattern-avoidance lemmas together with the row- and
column-degree facts; the four chains total $10{,}597$ propagation steps. We
state this explicitly because an earlier draft of this paper described each case
as closed by ``a short argument'', and that was wrong.

\textbf{Those $588$ lemmas are not additional inputs.} Each is a
\emph{relabelled copy} of $\Pi_8$ or $\Pi_{14}$ under the order-$392$ group, and
the count decomposes as $588 = 196 + 392$: the incidence matrix of $\Pi_8$ is
symmetric under transposition, so the $P\leftrightarrow Q$ swap contributes
nothing new for it, while $\Pi_{14}$ gives the full $392$. So ``two patterns
suffice'' survives intact: the library is the two theorems of
\S\ref{sec:patterns} instantiated, not new mathematics.

So Theorem~\ref{thm:cover} should be read as machine-checked, with the $28$-fold
case split being the part a reader can survey. What the reduction buys is not
that the whole argument became hand-checkable, but that its \emph{inputs} did:
two pattern non-realizability facts, one of which is
Definition~\ref{def:pat8}.
\end{remark}

\section{Excluding 40 and 42}\label{sec:exclude}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:not4042}
No configuration of two \emph{simple} heptagons has $I=40$ or $I=42$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Immediate from Theorems~\ref{thm:pat8}, \ref{thm:pat14} and~\ref{thm:cover}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:38}
Every configuration of two \emph{simple} heptagons has $I\le 38$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
$I\le42$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:42}, $I$ is even by Lemma~\ref{lem:even}, and $40$
and $42$ are excluded by Corollary~\ref{cor:not4042}.
\end{proof}

\section{From orientation signs to the plane}\label{sec:plane}

Sections~\ref{sec:patterns}--\ref{sec:exclude} are about sign vectors. Three
further steps are needed to reach Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, and all three are
carried out unconditionally.

\subsection{Integer coordinates are no restriction}

The combinatorial development uses integer coordinates. This costs nothing.
Fix a configuration $V$ with all $364$ signs nonzero. Each sign condition is a
strict inequality in the $28$ coordinates, so the set of tuples with the same
sign vector is open and contains a product of coordinate intervals around $V$,
say of width $\delta>0$. Since $\orient$ is homogeneous of degree~$2$ that set
is also scale-invariant, so $tV$ is good for every $t>0$ and the corresponding
product of intervals around $tV$ has width $t\delta$. Choose $t>1/\delta$: every
coordinate interval then has length exceeding~$1$ and so contains an integer,
and rounding each coordinate independently lands on an integer point with the
same $364$ signs. No rationals and no clearing of denominators are required.

\subsection{Counting points, not side pairs}

The combinatorial argument counts crossing \emph{pairs}; the theorem counts
\emph{points}. Under Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:e9} --- \emph{not} under full general
position, which Theorem~\ref{thm:main} does not assume --- these agree: a
crossing pair meets in exactly one point, and conversely a shared point lying on
a pair with a vanishing determinant would put a vertex on the other boundary,
which (E9) forbids. The chain is then
\[
  \#\text{points}(V) \;=\; \#\text{pairs}(V)
  \;\le\; \#\text{pairs}(V') \;\le\; 38,
\]
where $V'$ is a perturbation as in \S\ref{sec:removecollin}. Both equalities and
both inequalities are proved.

\subsection{Removing the collinearity assumption}\label{sec:removecollin}

\cite[\S3]{AckermanKeszeghRote2022} assumes, ``for convenience'' and without proof, that no three
of the combined vertices are collinear. We do not assume it. The argument is a
perturbation, in two halves:

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:open}
Crossing is an open condition, so for a small enough perturbation of the
fourteen vertices no existing crossing is lost.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:simple}
If two segments are disjoint, they remain disjoint when their four endpoints
move by less than some $\varepsilon>0$.
\end{lemma}

Lemma~\ref{lem:open} handles the crossings; Lemma~\ref{lem:simple} handles
simplicity. These need \emph{different} arguments: for a \emph{given} pair of
sides, ``does not cross'' is a closed condition, so it cannot be inherited from
the openness of crossing that gives Lemma~\ref{lem:open}. Simplicity is
nonetheless stable, because for a polygon simple as a point set the
non-adjacent closed sides are disjoint compact sets and hence at positive
distance --- which is what Lemma~\ref{lem:simple} exploits. The configurations in general position are dense: the complement is the zero
set of finitely many nonzero polynomials in the $28$ coordinates, hence a finite
union of hypersurfaces. Combining this with the two lemmas approximates an
arbitrary pair of point-set simple heptagons by one in general position with at
least as many crossings, giving the bound for the original. A single
$\varepsilon$ must serve every crossing, every disjoint side pair and all $364$
determinants at once; since there are finitely many conditions, one takes the
minimum.

One case deserves separate mention, since Lemma~\ref{lem:simple} does not cover
it: \emph{adjacent} sides share an endpoint and so are never disjoint.
Simplicity constrains them too --- they must meet only at that shared vertex ---
and this is exactly the delicate case, because three consecutive collinear
vertices are what \S\ref{sec:removecollin} exists to permit. In the formal
development the adjacent case is handled separately, by the observation that
once the perturbed configuration is in general position its adjacent sides
cannot overlap. Lemma~\ref{lem:simple} itself is proved for an arbitrary real
normed space and is independent of everything else here.

\begin{remark}
Note the direction: we need the perturbed configuration to have \emph{at least}
as many crossings, not the same number, and openness gives exactly that.
\end{remark}

\section{What is imported}\label{sec:import}

\begin{hypothesis}[\textsc{(E9)}]\label{hyp:e9}
No vertex of either polygon lies on the boundary of the other.
\end{hypothesis}

This is the conclusion of \cite[\S9]{AckermanKeszeghRote2022}, where \S9.1
removes vertex--vertex coincidences and \S9.2 vertex--side ones.

\begin{remark}[What is conditional, exactly]
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is itself \emph{unconditional}: like any theorem it has
hypotheses, and Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:e9} is one of them. What is conditional is
the \emph{deduction} of $f(7,7)=38$, and that needs a \emph{reduction}, not a
property. We state it with explicit quantifiers, since this is the precise
object we import.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Imported; \protect{\cite[\S9]{AckermanKeszeghRote2022}}]\label{prop:reduction}
For every pair $(P,Q)$ of simple heptagons with finitely many boundary
intersections there is a pair $(P',Q')$ of simple heptagons satisfying
Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:e9} with
$\lvert\partial P'\cap\partial Q'\rvert \ge \lvert\partial P\cap\partial Q\rvert$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{corollary}
Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction} together with Theorem~\ref{thm:main} gives
$f(7,7)=38$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Given any pair of simple heptagons whose boundaries meet in finitely many
points --- which is the range over which $f(7,7)$ is defined ---
Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction} produces one satisfying
Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:e9} with at least as many intersection points, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} bounds that by $38$; the bound is attained by
Proposition~\ref{prop:lower}.
\end{proof}

\noindent
Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction} is the \emph{only} thing we take on trust, and
we do not prove it. Three things should be said about it plainly.

\begin{remark}[Provenance]
We read \cite{AckermanKeszeghRote2022} in its journal version, which is the only
version containing \S9: the SoCG/arXiv version \cite{AckermanKeszeghRote2020}
ends at \S8 and contains no treatment of degeneracies at all. A referee checking
our citation against arXiv will not find the section.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[What \S9 says, in its own words]\label{rem:akrquote}
Since we ask the reader to accept that Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:e9} is what \S9
delivers, we quote it. \cite[\S9]{AckermanKeszeghRote2022} opens:

\begin{quote}
``We will now justify our general-position assumption that we introduced in
Sect.~3 stating that \emph{no vertex of one polygon lies on the boundary of the
other polygon}. That is, we will show that one can get rid of such degenerate
cases \emph{without decreasing the number of intersection points}.''
\end{quote}

\noindent
The second clause is exactly the inequality of
Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction}: the Proposition is their sentence, not our
paraphrase of it.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Their standing assumption is broader, and why that is harmless]
\cite{AckermanKeszeghRote2022} assume throughout that no three of the combined
vertices are collinear \emph{and} that no two of the combined sides are
parallel. We use neither. One might worry that \S9's own argument runs under
that full assumption, in which case Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction} would be
stronger than what \S9 delivers, and \S\ref{sec:removecollin} would buy nothing
at the level of the final claim.

It does not, and their \S3 is explicit about the order. It first states the
vertex condition, ``justified in Sect.~9 by a non-trivial perturbation''; and
only then, \emph{given} that condition,

\begin{quote}
``we assume \emph{for convenience} that no three vertices of $P$ and $Q$
combined lie on a line, and no two sides of $P$ and $Q$ combined are parallel.
Indeed, given that no vertex of one polygon lies on the other polygon, this can
be achieved easily by another small perturbation of the sides without changing
the intersection pattern.''
\end{quote}

\noindent
So \S9 is \emph{prior} to the collinearity and non-parallelism clauses rather
than dependent on them, and \S\ref{sec:removecollin} does remove the former from
the final claim. The latter we never use.

The quotation also shows that this second perturbation is \textbf{asserted and
not proved}: ``for convenience'', ``can be achieved easily'', no lemma number
and no argument. That is the clause \S\ref{sec:removecollin} discharges, and it
is why we treat it separately from \S9 rather than lumping both under ``general
position''.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The import is slightly more than \S9 states]\label{rem:simplicity}
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} assumes \emph{both} that the polygons are simple as point
sets \emph{and} Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:e9}, so the reduction must deliver both. That
the perturbed polygons remain \emph{simple} is, as far as we can tell, not
stated in \cite{AckermanKeszeghRote2022}. What we import is therefore \S9's
conclusion \emph{together with} simplicity-preservation, and the latter has no
published warrant we can point to. We flag it rather than absorb it silently:
it is the joint at which a previous attempt on this problem failed.
\end{remark}

Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction}, so understood, is the \emph{only} external
mathematical input to Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.

Why it is needed, precisely: without it, a point of
$\partial P\cap\partial Q$ can lie on \emph{no} crossing pair. Two sides that
merely touch share a point but have vanishing determinants, so the pair is not
counted, and the bound on pairs does not bound points. The failure is in the
unfavourable direction, which is why \S\ref{sec:removecollin} does not also
dispose of it.

\begin{remark}[Citation]
It would be an under-citation to attribute all of the usual general-position
package to \S9. \S9 gives Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:e9} and nothing more; the
collinearity clause has its only warrant in one unproved sentence of \S3, and
is discharged here (\S\ref{sec:removecollin}). Additionally, that the perturbed
polygons remain simple is stated nowhere in that paper. We fold simplicity into
Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:e9} deliberately, so that the gap is visible.
\end{remark}

\subsection{An attempt to remove it}\label{sec:e9attempt}

We attempted to prove Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:e9} and did not succeed. The
decomposition into its vertex--vertex and vertex--side halves is formalized, as
are two local lemmas, but the global reduction is not. Two negative
observations came out of the attempt and are worth recording, because they
close off the obvious routes:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Generic perturbation preserving proper crossings does \emph{not} imply
    that the point count is monotone.
  \item Naive displacement into the interior of a cone does not either.
\end{itemize}

Explicit coordinates exhibiting both failures are recorded in the project
repository; they are analytic and \emph{not} machine-checked. This is consistent with the case on which an earlier attempt of ours broke:
writing $\Cone_P(v)$ for the cone spanned at a vertex $v$ by its two incident
sides, the difficulty arises when $\Cone_P(v)\subseteq\Cone_Q(w)$, since then
displacing $v$ into the cone at $w$ can destroy the intersection point rather
than split it. None of this is evidence that \textsc{akr}'s
theorem is false, and we allege no error in it; what remains is substantial
unfinished work.

\section{Machine verification, and its limits}\label{sec:machine}

Every mathematical assertion in \S\S\ref{sec:prelim}--\ref{sec:plane} is
verified by the Lean~4 kernel. There is no \texttt{sorry}, no
\texttt{native\_decide}, and no project-specific axiom; every theorem depends on
at most the three standard axioms \texttt{propext},
\texttt{Classical.choice}, \texttt{Quot.sound}. Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:e9} is a
hypothesis of the statement, never an axiom.

\begin{remark}[What the kernel does not check]\label{rem:faithful}
The kernel proves theorems about definitions; it does not certify that the
definitions mean what we say. That $\partial P\cap\partial Q$ is modelled by the
right set, and that ``simple heptagon'' is modelled by the right predicate,
are judgements a human reader must make. This is, in our view, the principal
remaining risk, and it is not reducible by more computation.

One partial check is available and we have carried it out: the definitions,
applied to the explicit $38$-crossing configuration of
Proposition~\ref{prop:lower}, yield exactly $38$. A wrong definition would have
to be wrong in a way that still lands on that value.
\end{remark}

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

Dillencourt, Mount and Saalfeld~\cite{DillencourtMountSaalfeld1993} posed the
problem and settled the even cases. \v{C}ern\'y, K\'ara, Kr\'al', Podbrdsk\'y, Sot\'akov\'a and
\v{S}\'amal~\cite{CernyEtAl2003} gave bounds for odd cases and solved $k=5$,
explicitly leaving $k=\ell=7$ open. Ackerman, Keszegh and
Rote~\cite{AckermanKeszeghRote2022} obtained asymptotically optimal bounds;
their \S9 is what we import. G\"unther's preprint~\cite{Gunther2012} on odd $n$
was withdrawn by its author.

Both of our tools are standard. Encoding order-type or chirotope realizability
as a satisfiability problem and refuting it mechanically is well established,
including in combination with proof assistants. Framing extremal results in
terms of forbidden configurations is likewise standard. What we have not found
in the literature is either applied to crossing patterns of two simple
polygons, or to $f(m,n)$.

\begin{remark}[On novelty]\label{rem:novelty}
We searched for prior statements of the covering result and found none. We
record plainly that this is \textbf{weak evidence and not a novelty claim}: the search was bounded, the forward citations of
\cite{CernyEtAl2003} were not examined, and the relevant literature is small
enough that a result could sit in it under different vocabulary. A domain expert's judgement is not replaced by such a search.
\end{remark}

\section{Further work: the family \texorpdfstring{$f(7,n)$}{f(7,n)}}\label{sec:further}

We record an observation which suggests that the natural sequel to this work is
not $f(9,9)$ but the whole family $f(7,n)$, and which costs two lines.

$\GD$ has $m+n$ vertices and, by Lemma~\ref{lem:42}, no isolated ones. A graph
on $N$ vertices with $c$ connected components has at least $N-c$ edges, so
$\lvert \ED\rvert \ge (m+n)-c$ and, with \eqref{eq:link},
\[
  I \;\le\; mn-(m+n)+c .
\]
For $m,n$ both odd, $mn-(m+n)$ is odd while $I$ is even. Hence $c\le 4$ already
forces $I\le mn-(m+n)+3$, the conjectured value: \textbf{to prove the conjecture
for a given $(m,n)$ it suffices to bound the number of components of $\GD$
by~$4$.}

Now fix $m=7$. Every component of $\GD$ contains an edge and therefore a side of
$P$, and there are only seven of those, so $c\le 7$ \emph{automatically, for
every $n$}. Consequently
\[
  f(7,n)=6n-4 \text{ for all odd } n
  \quad\text{would follow from ruling out } c\in\{5,6,7\},
\]
three cases whose number does not grow with $n$. Theorems~\ref{thm:pat8},
\ref{thm:pat14} and~\ref{thm:cover} of this paper do exactly that for $n=7$:
$I=42$ forces $c\ge7$ and $I=40$ forces $c\ge5$.

This places the problem on a visible ladder. At $m=5$ only $c=5$ has to be
excluded --- a single case --- and that is precisely the range
\cite{CernyEtAl2003} settle, obtaining $f(5,\ell)=4\ell-2$ for every $\ell$. So
$m=7$ is the next rung above a solved problem, at three cases rather than one,
and this paper supplies its base case.

Two further remarks, one encouraging and one not.

\begin{remark}[The patterns are not about heptagons]
Neither Definition~\ref{def:pat8} nor Definition~\ref{def:pat14} mentions the
number of sides: $\Pi_8$ is a statement about eight points and three consecutive
sides of each polygon, and $\Pi_{14}$ about eleven. Theorems~\ref{thm:pat8}
and~\ref{thm:pat14} are therefore already theorems about $n$-gons for every $n$,
and would be reused unchanged. What does not transfer is
Theorem~\ref{thm:cover}, the covering claim, which is verified by enumeration
over a domain that depends on $n$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Why a single pattern cannot suffice, for any fixed $n$-free
argument]\label{rem:diagonal}
The case $c=7$ is rigid: each component holds exactly one side of $P$, so every
side of $Q$ fails to cross \emph{exactly one} side of $P$, giving a surjection
$\sigma$ from the sides of $Q$ onto those of $P$, where $\sigma(j)$ is the
unique side of $P$ that $Q_j$ misses. Consider the diagonal
$\sigma(j)\equiv j\pmod 7$, with $j$ taken in $\{0,\dots,n-1\}$.

Suppose $\Pi_8$ occurred on $P_i,P_{i+1},P_{i+2}$ and $Q_j,Q_{j+1},Q_{j+2}$.
The prescribed non-crossing at the centre gives $\sigma(j+1)=i+1$. If
$\sigma(j+1)=\sigma(j)+1$ in $\mathbb{Z}/7$, this forces $i=\sigma(j)$, so
$(P_i,Q_j)$ is a non-crossing as well --- contradicting the crossing that
$\Pi_8$ prescribes in that corner. \textbf{That last step is where an earlier
version of this remark overreached: $\sigma(j+1)=\sigma(j)+1$ holds for every
window except the one in which $j$ wraps from $n-1$ to $0$, and at that seam it
holds precisely when $7\mid n$.} So the argument kills every window except the
one at $j=n-1$ unconditionally, and that one only under a hypothesis on $n$.

The seam window is small enough to settle outright. There $j=n-1$ and
$\sigma(0)=0$, so the centre condition forces $i=6$; the remaining prescribed
crossings reduce to the single requirement $\sigma(n-1)\notin\{6,0,1\}$: the
remaining cells ask $\sigma(0)\notin\{6,1\}$ and $\sigma(1)\notin\{6,0\}$, and
$\sigma(0)=0$, $\sigma(1)=1$ satisfy those automatically, while the corner
$(P_1,Q_1)$ is the unconstrained $\ast$ of Definition~\ref{def:pat8}. Hence
\[
  \text{the diagonal avoids } \Pi_8
  \iff n\equiv 0,1,2 \pmod 7 .
\]
In particular the obstruction does hold whenever $7\mid n$, and it happens to
hold at $n=9$; but it \textbf{fails} at $n=11$ and at $n=13$, where the
diagonal does contain $\Pi_8$ --- in a single window read in the increasing
direction, the one straddling the seam, at $(i,j)=(6,10)$ and $(6,12)$
respectively. (Reading windows in the decreasing direction instead yields one
further copy at each of those $n$, also across the seam, and none at all when
$n\equiv 0,1,2$.) We confirmed the equivalence by direct enumeration for every
$n\le 120$ and both reading directions, with two independent implementations
and with controls confirming the search is not vacuous. The experiment recorded
in \texttt{independent/pattern\_scaling/} tests exactly this claim and reaches
the same conclusion; it adds that the $n=9$ diagonal, which $\Pi_8$ does miss,
is caught by $\Pi_{14}$.

The conclusion of the remark survives this repair, but on a restricted family
rather than at every $n$: the diagonal avoids $\Pi_8$ for every
$n\equiv 0,1,2\pmod 7$, which is infinitely many $n$ and infinitely many odd
$n$, so no argument for all $n$ can rest on $\Pi_8$ alone. It would need a
family of patterns closed under this kind of structure, or a different idea
altogether. We do not know whether a \emph{finite} family suffices for all $n$,
and if the number required grows with $n$ then this route fails.
\end{remark}

\noindent
We make no claim that any of this is easy. The point is only that the
obstruction is identifiable and the target is a single, finitely stated
combinatorial question, and that a general argument --- not an enumeration ---
would be needed, since ``all $n$'' is not something a computation reaches.

\section{Status, and what we ask of a reader}\label{sec:status}

To state the position exactly:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item $f(7,7)=38$ is \textbf{not} proved. Theorem~\ref{thm:main} carries
    Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:e9}, and the deduction needs
    Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction}, which we import.
  \item Every mathematical assertion in \S\S\ref{sec:prelim}--\ref{sec:plane} is
    machine-checked from primitive definitions, with no published result about
    $f(m,n)$ appealed to.
  \item \textbf{No mathematician outside this process has reviewed any of it.}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What can be judged without a proof assistant}

The third point is the binding constraint and is why this paper exists. We
stress that \textbf{no familiarity with Lean, or with formal verification at
all, is needed to assess the parts where an expert's judgement is
irreplaceable.} It is worth being precise about why.

The machine-checked proofs in this work are \emph{not} human-readable and are
not meant to be: they are propagation chains of thousands of steps, generated
rather than written. Nobody reads them --- a formalization specialist would not
either. What any reader of a formal development actually inspects is the
\emph{statements} and the \emph{definitions}, and we have put both into ordinary
mathematical notation, the definitions in Appendix~\ref{app:defs}. So the
practical difference between a Lean-literate reader and any other is close to
nil.

Three questions are the ones we would most value, and none of them involves a
machine:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Is Theorem~\ref{thm:pat8} true?} Eight incidences on three
    consecutive sides of each polygon, no simplicity hypothesis
    (Definition~\ref{def:pat8}). This is a question of plane geometry. We offer
    no human proof of it, so a synthetic argument --- or a counterexample ---
    would be decisive either way. \S\ref{sec:ot8} gives an independent
    verification by order-type enumeration that a reader can re-run in seconds
    without Lean, which is the nearest thing to a hand check we can supply. If it is false, the exclusion of $40$ and $42$
    collapses, though \S\ref{sec:prelim}, \S\ref{sec:lower} and
    \S\ref{sec:plane} would survive.
  \item \textbf{Does \cite[\S9]{AckermanKeszeghRote2022} actually deliver
    Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction}?} This is the one thing we import, and
    settling it is a matter of reading that paper, not this one. One worry we
    were able to resolve ourselves: \S9 does \emph{not} depend on AKR's
    standing non-parallelism assumption, because their \S3 introduces \S9's
    vertex condition \emph{before} that clause and derives the clause from it
    (see the remark on their standing assumption in
    \S\ref{sec:import}). What remains open is narrower and is recorded in
    Remark~\ref{rem:simplicity}: \S9 delivers the intersection-count
    inequality, but that the perturbed polygons remain \emph{simple} is not
    stated there, and we import it alongside.
  \item \textbf{Is the covering result (Theorem~\ref{thm:cover}) already known?}
    Our search found nothing, and we make no novelty claim
    (Remark~\ref{rem:novelty}).
\end{enumerate}

We would also welcome an opinion on Appendix~\ref{app:defs} --- whether those
definitions are the intended objects --- which is the risk
Remark~\ref{rem:faithful} identifies and the one thing no amount of computation

\section{The formal definitions, in ordinary notation}\label{app:defs}

Remark~\ref{rem:faithful} identifies definitional faithfulness as the principal
residual risk: the kernel proves theorems \emph{about} these definitions and
cannot certify that they are the intended objects. That judgement is a reader's,
so we set them out here. These are transcriptions of the formal statements, not
paraphrases; indices are mod $7$ and $[x,y]$ denotes the closed segment.

Let $P,Q:\mathbb{Z}/7\to\mathbb{R}^2$.

\paragraph{Simple as a point set.} $P$ is \emph{simple} if
\begin{enumerate}[label=(S\arabic*),leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item $P_i \ne P_{i+1}$ for every $i$ \quad(no degenerate side);
  \item $[P_i,P_{i+1}]\cap[P_j,P_{j+1}]=\emptyset$ whenever $P_i$ and $P_j$ are
    non-adjacent sides;
  \item $[P_i,P_{i+1}]\cap[P_{i+1},P_{i+2}]=\{P_{i+1}\}$ for every $i$.
\end{enumerate}
Nothing here constrains collinearity: three consecutive vertices of $P$ may be
collinear, and so may any three of the fourteen.

\paragraph{The hypothesis (E9).} Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:e9} is
\[
  P_i \notin [Q_j,Q_{j+1}]
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  Q_j \notin [P_i,P_{i+1}]
  \qquad\text{for all } i,j .
\]

\paragraph{What is counted.} The boundaries and their intersection are
\[
  \partial P=\bigcup_{i}[P_i,P_{i+1}],\qquad
  \partial Q=\bigcup_{j}[Q_j,Q_{j+1}],\qquad
  X = \partial P\cap\partial Q ,
\]
genuine subsets of $\mathbb{R}^2$, and the quantity bounded is the cardinality
of $X$. Finiteness of $X$ is \emph{proved}, not assumed --- it follows from the
injection of $X$ into the set of crossing side pairs --- which matters, because
the formal cardinality function returns $0$ on infinite sets and a bound stated
without finiteness would be vacuous for overlapping boundaries rather than
informative.

\paragraph{The theorem.} With $P,Q$ simple in the above sense and (E9) holding,
$\lvert X\rvert\le 38$; and $38$ is attained, by
Proposition~\ref{prop:lower}.

\medskip
\noindent
The question we are asking is simply whether (S1)--(S3), (E9) and $X$ above are
the objects a geometer means by ``two simple heptagons whose boundaries meet in
$k$ points''. If they are not, no amount of machine checking helps.
